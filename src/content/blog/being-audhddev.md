I was in the pipeline to be diagnosed with a personality disorder until COVID happened. That pause — unexpected as it was — created space for a different kind of reckoning, and eventually a diagnosis that actually fit: Autism and ADHD. AuDHD.

This isn't a pity post. It's more like a field report from someone who's been figuring out how to be a developer with a brain that works differently.

## The Hyperfocus Problem (and Advantage)

Hyperfocus is the AuDHD experience that most people find impressive, right up until they don't. The ability to sink completely into a problem — to lose four hours to debugging something that probably should have taken forty minutes — can look a lot like dedication from the outside.

And it genuinely is useful. Some of my best coding sessions have been hyperfocus episodes. I've built prototypes in an evening that I'd have estimated at a week. I've untangled genuinely gnarly problems because I simply couldn't stop pulling at the thread.

The flip side is that hyperfocus isn't voluntary. I can't reliably summon it for the task that actually needs doing. It arrives for the interesting thing, often at the expense of the important thing. The interesting thing might be exploring a new npm package when I should be writing tests. The important thing rarely gets the same mental energy.

I've had to build systems to compensate. Putting the "important but boring" thing at the top of my todo list before I'm allowed to touch the interesting thing. Timers. Explicit commitments to finish X before starting Y. None of it is perfect, but it reduces the damage.

## Bigmoji Was Born from Hyperfocus

[Bigmoji](/blog/bigmoji/from-emojilover-to-bigmoji-creator) is a perfect example. My team wanted a way to create multiline emoji art for Slack. A reasonable person might have Googled for an existing tool, maybe adapted something, and moved on in an hour.

Instead, I spent several evenings building a web app from scratch — my first real JavaScript project outside of tutorials. I explored the problem space thoroughly, got completely lost in the details of image slicing algorithms, and emerged with something genuinely useful. And I learned a lot doing it.

Was that the most efficient use of time? Probably not. Was it exactly the kind of thing hyperfocus enables? Absolutely.

## Executive Dysfunction Is the Real Boss

What people don't talk about as much is executive dysfunction — the difficulty of *starting* tasks, especially ones that feel large or uncertain. For me, this is the harder part of being an AuDHD developer.

A new project with no clear entry point can sit in my head as a vague obligation for an uncomfortably long time. The solution I've found is decomposition: break it down until there's a task so small it feels almost trivially easy to start. "Write the authentication system" is paralysing. "Create the users table migration" is fine.

This is probably why I write a lot of documentation and plans before touching code on larger projects. It's not just good engineering practice — it's also a mechanism for making the work feel navigable.

## Sensory Considerations in a Dev Environment

Open offices are genuinely difficult. The background noise, the unpredictable interruptions, the inability to control the environment — these are not minor inconveniences, they're significant cognitive costs. Working from home (or in a very quiet environment) isn't a preference, it's a meaningful productivity difference.

Noise-cancelling headphones are not optional for me. A clean, uncluttered workspace matters more than most people would expect. These aren't aesthetic preferences; they're accommodations.

## The Communication Thing

I communicate directly. Sometimes people read directness as bluntness or lack of social awareness. I'm aware this is a thing, and I try to add softening language where I know it's expected, but I also don't always catch it in the moment.

In code reviews, I've had to learn to frame feedback more carefully than feels natural. My instinct is to say "this is wrong because X." The received version of that is often harsher than I intended. Working on this — not changing who I am, but being more deliberate about tone in async written communication — is ongoing.

The upside of communicating directly is that I say what I mean and I mean what I say. There's no subtext to decode. In technical contexts, this is often exactly what's needed.

## What Works for Me

After several years of figuring this out:

- **Written over verbal** — for anything complex, I process better with time to read and think
- **Async over sync** — meetings are high-friction; a well-written document or issue comment is much more effective
- **Structure in daily routine** — the routine itself reduces cognitive load for the boring decisions, saving capacity for the actual work
- **Leaning into the interests** — the projects I've built have almost all started from genuine curiosity or a problem I personally had. That's not coincidence; that's how AuDHD works best

I'm still working it out. The goal isn't to overcome the neurodivergence — it's to build a way of working that makes the most of how my brain actually operates.

If you're reading this and you recognise yourself in it: you're not broken, you're just wired differently. Find the accommodations that work for you and don't apologise for needing them.
